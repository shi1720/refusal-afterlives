When a romance game offers a refusal, what exactly remains available to be chosen? This paper examines the public first episode of Affairs of the Court: Choice of Romance, a fantasy of courtship, family advancement, and royal power. Combining close reading with executable source analysis, it distinguishes a protagonist's expressed intention, the program's memory of that intention, and access to a continuing story.

One courtship menu lets players withhold thanks either to encourage pursuit or because they want pursuit to stop. These opposed motives execute identical narration, relationship updates, and destinations. Source comparison establishes their equivalence; 5,288 paired replays find no downstream narrative-state differences. Exhaustive analysis of a bounded gift-and-intimacy passage identifies 43 choice sequences, verified across 17,372 executions against an independent transition model. Earlier acceptance of gifts determines whether returning a later gift stops pursuit or prompts another offer. Meanwhile, declining intimate acts can preserve the possibility of marriage. At the episode boundary, fulfilled nonroyal relationships can close the narrative while royal involvement enables serial continuation.

I call this asymmetric refusal memory: willingness and reluctance need not leave equally durable traces in an interactive romance. Rather than treating every branch as freedom or every constraint as failure, the paper asks whose desires the program remembers and which futures remain narratable. It contributes a reproducible method for reading consent mechanics alongside romance's promises of hopeful transformation, with practical applications for narrative editing and localization. Choosing love emerges as a temporal negotiation over recognition, attachment, and the possibility of choosing otherwise.
